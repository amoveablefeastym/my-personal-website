<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-8 mb-6 select-none">1</div>

She woke to the stream of sunshine falling on her face. Will had already left. She had developed a strange knack for telling time without a clock. She woke up at the same time yesterday anyway. The sunlight cast the same shapes on the wall, carried the same tepid warmth. The air in this room smelled eternally stale. Yet she couldn't tell which day it was.

Wednesday. She suddenly remembered she was not allowed to stay in bed forever today. A dinner party. For whom? She didn't remember the names, only that it was for Will's people—friends, colleagues, or clients.

She got out of bed quickly, the chill of the floor chasing away lingering sleep.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">2</div>

She no longer needed to ask the staff to locate every item on the shelves with precision. She quickly went through everything they needed tonight in mind. Vanilla extract, shelf D. Chicken, turn right, head to the freezer section. Greet the staff. Ask for the freshest piece.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">3</div>

The earliest guest arrived thirty minutes late.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">4</div>

"Traffic was brutal," Grace explained, stepping inside with a bottle of wine clutched in one hand.

"Thank you, Grace. It looks lovely," she barely glanced at the label. It would join the half-dozen other bottles lined up on the counter, unopened.

"So, Henry and I finally decided to redo the kitchen," Grace announced. "Quartz countertops, heated floors. I told him, if we're going to host, I want it to feel like we're in Architectural Digest."

"Good luck with that," someone said. "Those renovations always take twice as long as they say."

"Oh, we know," Grace laughed. "But what can you do? A house like ours needs upkeep."

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">5</div>

She stayed quiet, pushing a piece of eggplant around her plate. She sometimes wondered if these people were just NPCs in a game. Otherwise, why did they always have the exact same tasks and talk about the exact same things?

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">6</div>

"And what about you two? Have you thought about renovating? Maybe updating this place a bit?"

Will chuckled, dabbing at his mouth with his napkin. "She's the one with all the ideas. I just sign the checks."

Laughter.

"And kids? When are we going to see little ones running around here?"

Laughter.

She felt her stomach tighten. Her gaze flickered to Will, who didn't look up from his plate.

"Oh, you know how it is," she said lightly. "We've been so busy."

"Well, Will has wanted kids so much since he was in college! We used to bet he would be the first to be a dad."

Laughter.

She had never heard about this.

"Speaking of kids, Tim's been a handful lately. The school called again about his behavior. Henry says it's just a phase, but honestly, I'm not sure..."

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">7</div>

It was past midnight when she finished cleaning up. The dishes were washed, the counters wiped, the last wine glass dried and returned to its place. She dragged herself up the stairs, every step felt heavy. In the bedroom, Will was already in bed.

He turned slightly as she entered, his back soon facing her.

"Good night." Will mumbled. "You did such a wonderful job."

She had heard him say the exact same words to his secretary when she had completed a satisfactory task. He used to at least add in "baby" or something. Five minutes later, the snoring began.

She wondered why he could always sleep so fast and soundly while she stared into the pitch-black night in this room for hours. She wondered why he didn't talk to her about that awkward moment at the dinner party, why he just left her like this.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">8</div>

She wondered a lot of things about her husband.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">9</div>

When is the next dinner party?

What will she do when she wakes up tomorrow?

What is the next goal?

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">10</div>

She couldn't sleep.

It was always days like this that made her realize how little she truly knew about Will's life before her.

It was going to be a long night. She needed a little project.

She got out of bed quietly, and padded toward the study. The door creaked as she pushed it open, the familiar scent of old paper and dust greeting her. Her gaze fell on the faint outline of the desk by the window. The desk was his, a worn-out antique piece, scattered with papers and books he claimed to be working through.

She walked over and ran her fingers over the desk's surface, brushing past the edges of notebooks and folders. Her hand paused on a stack of envelopes. She untied the stack, flipping through them absently, until her eyes landed on a single envelope tucked toward the back. It wasn't part of the neat stack; it was loose, its edges slightly creased, and its presence felt oddly significant.

Her fingers stilled as she read the name on the return address.

*Rebecca.*

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">11</div>

She recognized it, though it took a moment to place it. Rebecca. That was the name he'd mentioned once or twice, offhandedly, during one of their early conversations. Rebecca, the woman he'd traveled with. Rebecca, the one who "taught him a lot about art and life" but who he said was "never the right fit."

*What a snob.* She thought. *Who still writes letters in the twenty-first century?*

*Will does.*

After their third date, he'd sent her a letter. She remembered sitting on her bed with her housemate, both of them cracking up over its contents. He'd written, in earnest, about how he was "drowning in her eyes" and how she had "a voice that could silence cities."

"He's a poet now?" she choked up from laughing. "What's next? Is he going to show up outside my window with a sonnet?"

And he did. Not a sonnet, exactly, but he had turned up beneath her apartment with a book of poetry in hand, reading some forgotten verse about love and destiny under the dim glow of the streetlight. It was ridiculous.

Three months later, they were married.

Their wedding had been simple. Nothing extravagant, just a gathering of family and close friends. She still recalled how his hands trembled slightly when he slipped the ring onto her finger, how his voice cracked just a little when he vowed to love her for all his days.

They used to take trips on weekends together, stopping at small diners and motels with neon signs flickering against the night sky. He made a point to stop at every used bookstore along the way. He kept a notebook filled with sketches of places they passed, scribbled thoughts in the margins about the people they met.

She remembered the little moments that made up their life together. Her head on his shoulder as they watched a thunderstorm roll in from their porch, the way he always made sure her car had a full tank of gas, the late-night grocery runs just because she craved ice cream, how he always left the porch light on when she worked late.

Will's anachronism had charmed her in a quite mystical way, since she had never liked the things that meant so much to him. He seemed so different from everyone else she'd dated, or anyone she met at work, the ones in stiff suits who measured time in sales quotas and client meetings, who could sell anything to anyone but rarely seemed to believe in anything themselves. In the pharmaceutical industry, they convinced her that productivity and sales were king. Will was never loud or flashy or overly polished. He loved art, books, music that no one played anymore. He wrote her letters—ridiculous letters, but heartfelt. She had never set foot in his study except when he asked her to clean it, and Will had never tried to pull her into his world. For this she appreciated him.

But she hadn't seen him pick up a book for at least a year now. She didn't know if it was because of her, or just the way life was.

She sat down at the desk, the envelope in her hand, and stared at it for a long moment.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">12</div>

<div class="not-prose font-serif italic text-[#333] dark:text-gray-300 space-y-4 leading-relaxed my-6">
  <p>Dear Will,</p>
  <p>Forgive the suddenness of this letter. It feels strange to write to you after so many years. I had a dream about you last night. I heard if you didn't end things with someone properly, they come back to haunt you. I certainly hope you're not doing this on purpose.</p>
  <p>The world has been spinning wildly since we last spoke. Anyway, I happen to be in New York for a few days. If you'd like to meet, I'll be at the café on Sunday.</p>
  <p>Best,<br/>Rebecca</p>
</div>

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">13</div>

The words blurred together.

<em>I'll be at the café on Sunday.</em>

Three years ago.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">14</div>

What had she been doing that Sunday? Those days were a haze. Brunches, errands, dinner parties. She was somehow hurrying through days and nights and not moving at all at the same time.

Had he gone? Had he sat across from Rebecca, speaking in that language of theirs, the one she never quite understood? Arts and novels and who was still worth reading after all these years.

She could have just asked him. Could have waited until he wandered into the study, pretended to be sorting through old papers, then looked up casually— *Hey, I found this letter of yours. Did you reply? Did you go?* They had been married for eight years. She should be able to say anything to him. That's what marriage was, wasn't it?

But she just couldn't.

She pulled out his pen and paper, and began to write.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">15</div>

<div class="not-prose font-serif italic text-[#333] dark:text-gray-300 space-y-4 leading-relaxed my-6">
  <p>Dear Rebecca,</p>
  <p>Hi. How are you doing? I'd love to hear more about your life. Where have you been?</p>
  <p>As for me—well, life has been steady. Predictable in a way that should be comforting, and perhaps it is. But sometimes, I wonder about the roads not taken. Not regret, exactly. Just curiosity.</p>
  <p>Write me back. Tell me about the world as you see it.</p>
  <p>Will</p>
</div>

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">16</div>

Was this how he would write? She imagined his sweet little mind, cracking into it for the right balance between an ethical gentleman and his carefully hidden, longing flirtation. Imagining himself to be Fitzgerald, no doubt.

Fitzgerald knew how love could shift. She wondered what he would write about their marriage. Would he write about how he still tapped his fingers on the counter, but she no longer looked up to meet his eyes. About how the impromptu dances faded, replaced by quiet nods as they passed each other in the hallway.

No. It's that time to think about this. She looked at the return address, it was from a town that she had never heard about before. Is it some faraway place? Or is Rebecca just 30 minutes car ride from them?

<em>Quick. Send it. Buy the stamps. Lick the envelope. Put the letter inside. Drop it into the post. Mail it to the address written on the envelope. Does Rebecca still live here? Doesn't matter. Quick. Just send it.</em>

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">17</div>

Rebecca's reply arrived sooner than she expected.

<div class="not-prose font-serif italic text-[#333] dark:text-gray-300 space-y-4 leading-relaxed my-6">
  <p>Dear Will,</p>
  <p>I was surprised to hear from you. I'm glad you wrote to me. The world is a better place with dorks like us who still appreciate this long-lost art.</p>
  <p>I'm doing well. I guess.</p>
  <p>You asked where I've been. Let's see.</p>
</div>

And then Rebecca told her, or Will, about the past ten years that she spent without Will.

Rebecca had been sketching along the Seine. That same year she and Will bought their first washing machine. She remembered how the delivery had come late, how Will had been stuck at work, how she had pulled a muscle dragging the damn thing inside by herself. That night, she had taken painkillers and ordered takeout. She had gone to bed early.

The summer Rebecca spent in Lisbon, they had bent to Will's mother's pressing, and decided to start trying for a baby. Will had suggested that she quit her job.

Rebecca spent an entire winter learning skiing in Colorado. The same winter that Will's mother got sick. She learnt how to care for her. How to clean bedpans and lift a frail, unsteady body into a bath without slipping or letting the skin bruise. How to be patient when she felt anything but.

Rebecca fell in love with a woman, but that burned into flame together with the fireworks on the fourth of July. In that month, Will's mother had died. She was supposed to feel grief, or something of that sort. But she just felt so refreshed from the whole night's sleep, the first one she had gotten since Will's mother got sick. Maybe that's why she looked radiant at the funeral. She had to put the lightest shade of foundation on her face so that she would look the part of a grieving daughter-in-law. That was the day she found out she was pregnant.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">18</div>

She woke up extra early the next day. Not for grocery shopping. Not because she had to.

She decided to take a walk.

The morning air was crisp, the streets quiet except for the occasional dog walker or early commuter. She took the longer route; past the little park she always drove by but never stepped into. The benches were wet with dew.

She stopped by the florist and left with a bouquet of wildflowers—lavender, daisies, tiny sprigs of something purple she couldn't name. Not the clean, sophisticated arrangement people usually placed on the dining table, but something messier, something Rebecca might have picked herself from a field in Lisbon or Paris.

She took her time arranging them. She placed them by the window, where the light caught their colors just right. She never got any flowers for their home, but now she felt that they needed them.

She sat down at Will's desk.

Rebecca's letter laying in front of her. She read it again, tracing each line.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">19</div>

What should she say?

What would Will say?

She didn't have to reply. The letter seemed to have told her that they never met up. She had already got her answer. She could burn the letter. She could shove it back into the envelope and forget she ever saw it.

She was never good with writing. She lacked imagination.

Even their wedding vows—she remembered sitting in front of a blank page the night before, staring at the emptiness, while Will scribbled effortlessly beside her.

"I'm terrible at this," she had told Will.

Will had smiled and said, "Just tell the truth."

So now, she picked up his pen and tried to do just that.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">20</div>

She thought back to the past ten years. Nothing came to mind but her marriage.

She wrote about their marriage from his perspective. About the mornings when they passed each other in the hallway, barely speaking beyond the necessary words. How their conversations had dwindled into logistics—groceries, bills, whether they should replace the old carpet. How Will had given up reading except for his self-help books. How she couldn't quite pinpoint when silence had become their default.

She even wrote about their child, the one who was never born.

She didn't know why she did it, why she told Rebecca, of all people. She wrote about the moment they found out. How she sat in the doctor's office, staring at the ultrasound screen. The doctor's soft, rehearsed words. In Will's words, of course.

She sealed the envelope.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">21</div>

<div class="not-prose font-serif italic text-[#333] dark:text-gray-300 space-y-4 leading-relaxed my-6">
  <p>Dear Will,</p>
  <p>I'm sorry to hear about your child. It must have been hard on your wife.</p>
  <p>If something never happened, maybe it wasn't meant to be. Or maybe it just wasn't the right time. I don't know if that makes it easier or harder to accept. Sometimes, we make choices without realizing we've made them at all. And those choices opened up a new life for us. Please tell her that.</p>
  <p>Rebecca</p>
</div>

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">22</div>

The words Will never said to her, she heard from Will's ex-girlfriend.

Will had told her to get some rest. And then he never brought it up again. Never asked her how she felt or if she wanted to talk about it.

Maybe he thought silence was kindness. Maybe he thought if he didn't say it, if he let time fold over it, the loss would settle like dust on a shelf, forgotten until it no longer hurt.

She didn't even know how she felt. She wasn't sure if she had wanted the baby or not. It had just felt like… time. The logical next step, the thing you do when you've been married long enough and the people around you start asking *so when will you have one?* She hadn't fought it. She hadn't fought it when Will asked her to quit her job when they started trying.

But it was like—having the baby meant *this is it.* This is her life. Forever. No more questions, no more wondering if she had made the right choices. Of course it's right, it's a baby, her baby, it'll be her life, right? The path would be set. She just wasn't ready to admit that.

And then, it was gone.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">23</div>

She wondered if she had killed the child with her own hesitation. If her body had simply rejected what her mind had not yet accepted. The child was never born, but she never went back to work, either. At first, she told herself she just needed time to recover, that she'd return when she was ready. But it was just… easier to stay where she was. Days turned into weeks. Then months. Then years. And then, one day, she woke up and realized she wasn't the woman who could charm a room full of doctors, who could recite clinical studies from memory and push a hesitant client toward a signature with nothing but a well-timed pause. Now she found herself struggling through small talk; she had become rusty.

She hadn't expected Rebecca to say that—to acknowledge her. The wife. She hadn't talked to anyone like that in a while. All the friends she had when she was young had just drifted away after she got married. Just a slow fading. They got busy, she got busy. They stopped calling, and so did she. She had friends now—Will's friends. Couples they went to dinner with. Friends who knew her as Will's wife and nothing more. But with Rebecca, they talked. Although Rebecca was talking to Will.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">24</div>

<div class="not-prose font-serif italic text-[#333] dark:text-gray-300 space-y-4 leading-relaxed my-6">
  <p>Dear Rebecca,</p>
  <p>Hi.</p>
</div>

She paused. She didn't want to lie to Rebecca like this anymore. She should tell her. That it was her all along. Not Will. Not some distant, wistful ex-lover reaching across time. Just her. She owed Rebecca that honesty.

<div class="not-prose font-serif italic text-[#333] dark:text-gray-300 space-y-4 leading-relaxed my-6">
  <p>I don't really know how to begin this, so I'll just say it—I'm not Will. I'm married to Will. I'm Elena.</p>
  <p>I know this must be strange to read. I found your letter in our house by accident, and I don't know why I wrote back pretending to be him. At first, I thought I just wanted to know if he had gone to see you. But then your letters kept coming, and I kept replying.</p>
  <p>Thank you for showing me a life I never had the chance to experience. Sometimes, I wonder if I could have been you, if I had made different choices. And thank you for listening, for talking to me. Even though you may be talking to Will.</p>
  <p>I don't know if you'll ever want to write back after this, but I hope you do. Because, selfishly, I wish we could have more conversations like this.</p>
  <p>Elena</p>
</div>

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">25</div>

Rebecca never wrote back.

People got busy. Letters got lost. Rebecca could be out of town. Out on one of her own adventures. Elena thought. And, it should be what's expected. Why would Rebecca write back to her after she had lied to her?

Then, she began to imagine one morning, Rebecca toasting bread, the toaster sparking into flames, the fire swallowing her.

She imagined Rebecca stepping onto a cobblestone street in some sunlit European town, a bus hurtling around the corner, the driver distracted, the brakes failing.

She imagined Rebecca being struck by lightning in the middle of an open field, her body disappearing into smoke.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">26</div>

The next day, she found herself on the drive to Rebecca's address. She just told Will she had to visit her sister. He didn't ask much.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">27</div>

Rebecca's town was much smaller than she had imagined. The streets were peaceful and still, stretching out in slow, gentle curves, with no traffic, no bustling crowds, just the soft sound of her footsteps on the pavement.

There was a florist on the corner, the flowers almost too relaxed in their beauty. Next door, the bakery's window was fogged with warmth. The scent of freshly baked bread spread into the air, it was sweet and comforting.

There was no hurry here, no pressing reason to be anywhere. It seemed like the kind of place where everyone probably knew each other's name, and days slipped by without urgency.

Rebecca's address led her to a street shaded by old trees. The building was modest, the paint slightly chipped around the edges. There was a little café on the first floor. Its windows were open to the afternoon light, curtains shifting in the breeze. Elena hesitated. It felt absurd now, standing in front of Rebecca's door unannounced.

She knocked.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">28</div>

Rebecca stood there, barefoot, wrapped in a worn sweater. She seemed like she just woke up.

But it was indeed Rebecca, flesh and blood. No longer just ink on a page or a voice in her mind. The woman who had once only been a figment of Elena's wildest imagination.

"Who's this?" Rebecca seemed a little annoyed.

She hadn't thought this far ahead. "Oh… I'm here to… I work for the dog shelter. Have you thought about adopting a dog?"

"Oh, probably not. I already have a dog. He's been keeping me busy."

Elena hadn't expected that. She used to imagine if she was Rebecca, she wouldn't take on any responsibility, or anything to hold her in place. She would just be free.

"Oh really? How long have you had him?"

"Two years, I think."

She should leave, but she just wanted to talk to Rebecca.

"How has it been, keeping him?" She said. "And, uh, what do you do for work? You know, for… survey purposes."

"It's not easy sometimes," Rebecca said. "He's been keeping me at home. But it's probably what I need right now. And I like my job—I'm working as a barista in the café downstairs. And I like this town. And he's too cute to leave alone for long."

"How long have you been living here?" Elena said.

"Gosh… I don't know. Around five years now?"

Five years. Long enough for the life she had imagined for Rebecca, this dazzling, weightless existence, to become something else. She remembered that was the year that Rebecca sent Will that letter. Is that why she sent the letter? Because she moved to a new place and was scared and wanted to cling onto something, someone? But Elena found that she didn't care about that anymore. She just wanted to look at Rebecca properly.

Rebecca looked thirty-eight. And she was thirty-eight.

Maybe it was the way she carried herself, the way the corners of her eyes crinkled when she glanced toward the street, the way she stood. She seemed okay. She seemed really good.

"Thank you, Rebecca. I hope everything works out."

"Of course. You, too."

Rebecca closed the door.

<div class="text-5xl font-serif text-gray-200 dark:text-gray-700 text-center mt-16 mb-6 select-none">29</div>

Elena stopped by the cafe below Rebecca's apartment.

She imagined Rebecca here. She imagined her standing right behind the counter, by the window. How Rebecca rented a little place, found a job here, learned to be a barista from scratch. Probably got scolded by the manager for steaming the milk wrong or dismissed by customers when she fumbled with change. How she got better. How she learnt the way the beans smelled in the morning, the quiet satisfaction of a perfect pour.

A woman in her fifties was chatting with the barista, her laughter carrying over the soft hum of conversation. A man in paint-stained jeans sat in the corner, sketching something in a notebook. A couple was sharing a slice of cake. Elena traced the rim of her cup, the quiet clatter of ceramics and spoon blew through her ears.

Maybe she should stay here for a while. She could come back to the café in the morning, sitting by the window, and properly introduce herself to Rebecca.

Maybe she should really go work at that dog shelter. She had always liked dogs. She could manage the volunteer schedule, or be in charge of food purchase. She'd start waking up early, taking long walks to work, and remember every dog's name.

Maybe she should talk to Will. But she wouldn't know what to say or how to start. Maybe she should write him a letter instead. To say what? That she met Rebecca. That she hated the way he complimented her, and she hated the dinner parties and all his friends. That she felt like she had been sleepwalking through the past few years, how she didn't know when exactly she had stopped trying. She'd ask him if he noticed it too, if he had felt the silence creeping in the same way she had. She'd ask him why he had never talked to her about the baby, tell him her hesitation, her doubt, her guilt. She'd ask him to talk, just talk to her.

</div>

<div class="w-16 h-px bg-gray-300 dark:bg-gray-700 mx-auto mt-16 mb-2"></div>
