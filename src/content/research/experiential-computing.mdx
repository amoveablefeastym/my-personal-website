*Fall 2025.* WorldLearning began with a simple observation: people across cultures seek the same things (fun, connection, belonging, purpose) but realize them in radically different ways. The goal was to make these differences visible, and to encourage curiosity about the social, historical, and cultural factors that shaped them.
> People around the world share broad experiential
goals such as “relaxing” or “having fun,” but the concrete
activities that realize these experiences vary widely by place.
Existing tools like search engines, guidebooks, travel blogs, and
large language models (LLMs) can answer questions such as
“What do people do for fun in Japan?”, yet they typically present
one country at a time and require users to already know what
to ask. These answer-first interfaces make it hard to see how
a single experience is realized differently across countries, or to
surface surprising contrasts that might provoke deeper questions.
This paper presents WorldLearning, an interactive system that
uses an LLM and a world map to help users explore how local
people in different countries typically have fun in their free time.
Given a user-specified activity (for example, “going to karaoke”),
WorldLearning uses an LLM as a scoring model to estimate how
typical that activity is as something locals do for fun in each
country, and visualizes the resulting scores as a choropleth map
with ranking lists and follow-up explanation tools. The system
is designed to transform interaction with LLMs from one-shot
question–answer exchanges into a dialectical process in which
visualized patterns generate new questions that users then probe
further. A preliminary study with two participants engaging
in guided think-aloud exploration suggests that WorldLearning
can surface unexpected global patterns, prompt questions that
users would not have asked on their own, and support sustained
curiosity-driven inquiry about why everyday experiences are
realized differently across countries.

### Key Features

- **Interactive 3D Visualization:** A reactive globe built with Three.js allowing users to spin, zoom, and select countries to visualize data spatially.
- **LLM-Driven Scoring:** Uses GPT-4 to dynamically score the "typicality" of activities across different cultures, turning qualitative knowledge into quantitative data.
- **Comparative Analysis:** Side-by-side ranking lists and choropleth maps allow users to contrast how different regions prioritize specific experiences.
- **Dialectical Exploration:** Designed to prompt follow-up questions rather than just providing static answers, fostering curiosity-driven inquiry.

### Technical Stack

- **Frontend:** React (TypeScript), TanStack Start & Router, Tailwind CSS. D3.js and TopoJSON for map visualizations.
- **Backend:** Node.js via TanStack Start server functions. Zod for schema validation and custom middleware for auth.
- **AI / ML:** OpenAI API (davinci-002 for log-probs, GPT-4 for explanations). Custom tokenization and log-summation logic.
- **Tools:** Vite, pnpm, ESLint, Prettier.

As I continued my research, I began asking myself a different question.

**Is simply learning facts about differences enough? What were the moments that had actually changed me?**

The answer was never a statistic, a map, or a piece of information.


One of my best friends is from Cartagena, Colombia. I remember listening to him talk about home, the neighbors who stopped to greet each other, the music, the dancing, the way life seemed to spill out into the streets. There was a warmth, a passion, and a deep love in the way he remembered everything. Weirdly, I felt connected to a place I had never been.

But I couldn't reconcile with the contradiction:

**How could a country that had experienced so much violence, conflict, and collective trauma still be so full of joy? How could people continue to dance, sing, love, and celebrate after everything they had endured?**

At first, I thought I was trying to understand Colombia. Eventually, I realized I was trying to understand myself.

When faced with hardships in my own life, my instinct is often to move toward the pain, to make it bigger in my head than it actually is, to analyze it, revisit it, fetishize it. Yet the stories I heard from Colombia seemed to point toward a different form of resilience. Not the denial of suffering, but the refusal to allow suffering to become the entirety of one's life. Joy was not the absence of pain. It was a way of living alongside it.

For the first time, I began to wonder whether resilience was not found in endlessly revisiting pain, but in continuing to move despite it.

This realization led me to reconsider the purpose of the project. What stayed with me from encounters with other cultures was not factual knowledge, it was emotional transformation. The most meaningful moments were not when I learned something new about another place. They were when another person's way of living challenged my assumptions about my own.

Rather than treating places as destinations to be learned about, WorldMirror treats places as expressions of human values, priorities, and ways of responding to life's challenges. Each place represents a particular organization of social norms, relationships, histories, and environments. By exploring these systems, users are not only learning about others; they are encountering alternative ways of understanding belonging, community, resilience, and meaning.

Places become *models of life organization*, and ultimately mirrors through which users can examine their own assumptions and tensions.
> People are often limited to their own perspectives,
which are deeply shaped by their surroundings. Place – that
is, the geographic location in which people live and interact
with one another – is enriched by historical, cultural, and social
contexts which subsequently influences how people think and
behave. People often perceive locations, especially unfamiliar
ones, as inherently distinct from the self, merely as places to
visit and learn about rather than as mechanisms for personal
development. Yet, people’s internal worlds are shaped by the
same forces which shape surroundings. Individual habits, be-
liefs, values, and attitudes evolve through exposure to societal,
cultural, and environmental pressures. Whether consciously or
subconsciously, people make efforts to improve self-concept and
overcome personal challenges through reflection, self-awareness,
and seeing issues from a new perspective. Oftentimes, it is
the last element that enables the first two to occur. There
is an opportunity to reconcile people’s internal worlds with
their external world, particularly through providing people with
contexts that are disparate from their own. In this manner, we
welcome alternative perspectives, grounded by physical locations,
to help individuals reveal current thought patterns, make com-
parisons, and ultimately gain personal insights. In this paper,
we present WorldMirror, a system that uses a mixture of LLM
discussions and interaction with videos to help users gain a deeper
understanding of their problem, learn different ways to approach
their problem, and learn about the world. Given a user problem,
the LLM prompts the user to learn more about their problem
and connect it with different aspects of the user’s life to identify
underlying root causes. Then the LLM provides locations around
the world that can teach the user new ways of thinking about
their problem. Through interacting with a video of people living
their daily lives at the location and further discussion with the
LLM, the system pushes users to learn more about their problem
and the world. A preliminary study with one participant using
the system suggests that WorldMirror can understand the user’s
problem thoroughly and identify root causes but struggles to help
users grow in their own thinking about their problem and the
world.

In the original project, reflection emerged from comparing cultural differences. In WorldMirror, reflection becomes the central mechanism. Users are guided to connect what they observe in an unfamiliar place back to their own experiences. The goal is not simply to understand why a practice exists elsewhere, but to ask what that practice reveals about one's own values, desires, and worldview.

I began by believing that learning about the world meant discovering how people are different. Through conversations, travel, and reflection, I came to believe that the most powerful encounters with difference are the ones that reveal our shared humanity. The project shifted from helping users understand other cultures to creating opportunities for the kind of emotional and personal transformation that occurs when another person's story causes us to see ourselves differently.
